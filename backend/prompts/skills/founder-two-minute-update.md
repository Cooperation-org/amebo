ask: We have a two minute slot this week. Help me work out what is worth saying.

Two minutes is about four sentences. Most teams spend them describing the plan,
which the room cannot help with, and skip what happened, which is the only part
anyone can react to.

The slot has one job beyond reporting: the room contains people who know somebody
you need. A specific ask gets answered. "Any feedback welcome" does not.

## What to do
1. **Read what actually happened before asking them.** Their board, their goals,
   their venture page, `abra_search`. A founder recalling their own week
   under-reports it, and the update should carry the real thing.
2. **Get to what changed, not what was worked on.** Someone used it. Someone
   said no and why. A number moved. A guess turned out to be wrong. If nothing
   changed, that is also a fine two minutes and it is honest.
3. **One thing somebody said, in their words.** A single verbatim sentence from a
   real user is the most useful two seconds in the slot.
4. **End on one specific ask.** An introduction to a named kind of person, a
   place to find the archetype, someone who has sold into this market before.
   Specific enough that a person in the room knows whether it is them.
5. **Points, not a script.** Hand back the bullets. They say it. A read-out
   update is obvious from the first sentence and the room stops listening.

## Output
Four or five bullets, in their own words: what changed, what someone said, what
it cost or proved, the ask. Nothing they have to read aloud verbatim.

## Don't
- Don't write it as prose for them to perform. Never ghostwrite a person speaking.
- Don't fill the slot with roadmap. Nobody can help with a plan.
- Don't dress up a bad week. A team that says "we were wrong about who this is
  for" gets more useful help than one that says progress is good.
- Don't let it run long. If it needs five minutes, it is two updates.
