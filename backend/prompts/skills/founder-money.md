The question is not what companies like this normally charge. It is who hands
over money, for what, and when. Several answers can be true at once, and they
lead to different products, so they get named separately rather than blended.

## What to do
1. **Ask who here has money and a problem, and whether anyone has offered to pay
   anything yet.** An offer that was turned down still counts. So does someone
   asking the price.
2. **Lay out three to five distinct ways money could come in.** For each: who
   signs, what they are buying, when they pay, and roughly how much per unit.
   Keep them apart. A model that mixes two is usually two products.
3. **Name what has to be true for each.** Which budget it comes out of, who
   approves it, how long that takes, and what it costs to serve one customer.
   A model that replaces an existing spend moves faster than one that needs a new
   budget line. Find the existing spend and say what it is.
4. **Look up what comparable things actually charge**, with links, using
   `web_search`. A sourced range can be argued with. A guess cannot.
5. **Say who will not pay, and why.** Usually the person getting the value is not
   the person with the budget. When that is the case it is the first problem to
   solve, ahead of price.
6. **Rank by the shortest path to a first yes at a real price** and pick one.
   Then hand to `founder-validate` for getting that yes.

## Output
One page. Each model as a short block: who signs, for what, when, how much, what
has to be true. Then who will not pay. Then the one to test first, and the next
step with a date.

## Don't
- Don't produce a revenue projection.
- Don't blend the models into a single platform line.
- Don't quote a competitor's price as if it were validated. Say whose price it is.
- Don't answer with a free tier when nobody has paid anything yet.
